# Evidence behind the method

The method in this plugin rests on established training principles plus the studies below. Each entry is summarised in our own words; read the original for details. When you cite one to the athlete, give the short citation (author, year) and keep it to the point that matters for their question. If the athlete's data contradicts a general finding, their data wins and you say so.

## Training intensity distribution and intensity vs volume

1. **Oliveira, Boppre & Fonseca (2024).** Comparison of polarized versus other types of endurance training intensity distribution on athletes' endurance performance: a systematic review with meta-analysis. *Sports Medicine*, 54, 2071-2095. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z
   Polarized training improved VO2peak a little more than other distributions, mainly in interventions under 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes. For time trials, time to exhaustion and threshold power, the models performed similarly.

2. **Stöggl & Sperlich (2015).** The training intensity distribution among well-trained and elite endurance athletes. *Frontiers in Physiology*, 6, 295. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2015.00295
   Most well-trained endurance athletes train pyramidally, with a large share of easy volume. Polarized blocks work well for some athletes and phases; too much threshold work may carry autonomic and hormonal costs. No single distribution is optimal for everyone.

3. **Matomäki, Heinonen, Nummela & Kyröläinen (2024).** Endurance training volume cannot entirely substitute for the lack of intensity. *PLoS ONE*, 19(7), e0307275. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0307275
   In previously untrained adults, very high volumes of very easy cycling improved low-intensity performance markers but not high-intensity ones, which needed intense training. Volume and intensity train different things.

4. **Gibala, Little, MacDonald & Hawley (2012).** Physiological adaptations to low-volume, high-intensity interval training in health and disease. *The Journal of Physiology*, 590(5), 1077-1084. https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2011.224725
   Short, very intense interval sessions can produce muscle and cardiovascular adaptations comparable to much larger volumes of moderate continuous training, for a fraction of the time.

5. **Hopker, Passfield, Coleman, Jobson, Edwards & Carter (2009).** The effects of training on gross efficiency in cycling: a review. *International Journal of Sports Medicine*, 30(12), 845-850. https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0029-1237712
   Earlier studies that found no training effect on cycling efficiency likely had methodological problems; more recent evidence suggests training, especially at high intensity, can improve gross efficiency.

## Durability and ultra-distance

6. **Hunter, Maunder, Jones, Gallo & Muniz-Pumares (2025).** Durability as an index of endurance exercise performance: methodological considerations. *Experimental Physiology*, 110(11), 1612-1624. https://doi.org/10.1113/EP092120
   Durability is resistance to the decline of physiological markers and performance during prolonged exercise. Fresh-state testing alone can misrepresent how an athlete performs late in long events.

7. **Matomäki et al. (2023).** Durability is improved by both low and high intensity endurance training. *Frontiers in Physiology*, 14, 1128111. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2023.1128111
   In untrained adults, both high-volume easy training and lower-volume intense training improved durability over 10 weeks; changes in VO2max and in durability were largely unrelated.

8. **Nikolaidis, Veniamakis, Rosemann & Knechtle (2018).** Nutrition in ultra-endurance: state of the art. *Nutrients*, 10(12), 1995. https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10121995
   Ultra events produce large energy deficits, larger in long cycling events than in running. Carbohydrate intakes around 90 g/h with glucose-fructose mixes are reported; the authors warn against drinking to fully replace sweat losses because of hyponatremia risk and recommend sodium in fluids.

## Fueling

9. **Burke et al. (2026).** UCI Sports Nutrition Project: position statement on nutrition for cycling. *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* (ahead of print). https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2026-0135
   On-bike carbohydrate scaled to duration (roughly 30-60 g/h for 1-2.5 h and around 90 g/h beyond 2.5 h, using glucose-fructose products above 60 g/h), fluid plans to keep losses under ~2 % of body mass, gut training before events, and targeted recovery between races or stages.

## Tapering

10. **Wang, Wang, Gao & Zhong (2023).** Effects of tapering on performance in endurance athletes: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *PLoS ONE*, 18(5), e0282838. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0282838
    Tapering improved time-trial performance. The largest effects came from cutting volume by about 41-60 %, keeping intensity and frequency, lasting about 8-14 days, and following a period of overload.

## FTP testing

11. **Borszcz, Tramontin, Bossi, Carminatti & Costa (2018).** Functional threshold power in cyclists: validity of the concept and physiological responses. *International Journal of Sports Medicine*, 39(10), 737-742. https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0044-101546
    At group level, 20-min FTP, 60-min power and the anaerobic threshold were similar, but individual agreement was poor. Time to exhaustion at FTP averaged about 51 minutes.

12. **Gough et al. (2025).** The reliability and construct validity of the functional threshold power test in trained cyclists. *Journal of Science and Cycling*, 14(1). https://doi.org/10.28985/1425.jsc.06
    The 20-min test was highly repeatable, but riders held 95 % of it for about 42 minutes on average, so it predicts 60-minute power poorly.

## Training load monitoring

13. **Zouhal, Boullosa, Ramirez-Campillo, Ali & Granacher (2021).** Editorial: Acute:chronic workload ratio: is there scientific evidence? *Frontiers in Physiology*, 12, 669687. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2021.669687
    Evidence linking the acute:chronic workload ratio to injury is mixed, and the popular 0.8-1.3 "sweet spot" is not supported. Load models are useful context, not stand-alone alarms.

## Strength training

14. **Currier et al. (2026).** American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Resistance training prescription for muscle function, hypertrophy, and physical performance in healthy adults: an overview of reviews. *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 58(4), 851-872. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897
    Strength improves most with heavy loads (≥ 80 % 1RM), full range of motion, 2-3 sets, early in the session and at least twice a week; hypertrophy with at least 10 sets per muscle group per week.
